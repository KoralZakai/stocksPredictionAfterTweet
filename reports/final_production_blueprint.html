How the data is labeled (so models learn to short, too)

Every call is scored relative to the S&P 500 — and the label is signed, so a winning short is rewarded exactly like a winning long.

+1 Instrument BEAT the S&P in the predicted direction — a WIN (long or short).
−1 Instrument LAGGED the S&P (moved against the call) — a MISS.
0 Move was inside the noise band — neutral / not tradeable.

Each call also keeps its predicted direction (+1 = long, −1 = short), so a downstream model learns asymmetric behaviour — it can bet on a crash, not only a rally. (Shipped runs use a strict band = "beat or not"; the ±1 / 0 three-way tag is the recommended target for asymmetric training.)

The cheat-sheet for production

How to actually use this system — long, short, and how to not fool yourself.

DO

  • Trade broad "macro" tweets (wars, peace, tariffs, rates) — that's where the edge lives.
  • Short the exposed sectors (autos, oil, global exporters) when a targeted threat is tweeted — the short side works as well as the long.
  • Act fast, close by end of day. The signal peaks at 1 hour and fades — a sprint, not a hold.
  • Measure against the market (beat / lag the S&P), not raw gains — strips out lucky market-wide moves.
  • Keep the signed direction (predicted_trend +1 long / −1 short) with every call so models learn asymmetric behaviour.

DON'T

  • Don't stack a "prediction model" on top of the AI's call — it overfit and failed on fresh data.
  • Don't trust weekend / after-hours tweets blindly — the immediate reaction is distorted and thin.
  • Don't bet single-stock tweets yet — beating a company's own peers is near-random (only 84 to test).
  • Don't hold for a month expecting the tweet to keep working — that's just market beta.
  • Don't read one call as certainty — a single leg is ~50/50; the edge is the basket consensus.
How we made sure we weren't fooling ourselves. We split every tweet by date into Train → Validation → a sacred, untouched Test set (the most recent tweets). When we built a fancier model, it looked better and better in Validation — and then failed the final exam three separate times.
